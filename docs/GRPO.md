# Grouped Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) Summary 

## Introduction 

GRPO is a reinforcement learning method used to fine-tune large language models, particularly in domains that involve multistep reasoning. It builds on Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) but introduces a reward structure where correctness can be automatically verified, such as in math or logic. 

While PPO relies on human preferences or learned reward models to evaluate outputs, GRPO instead uses feedback from consistent learning and self-generated evaluations [1]. The model acts as its own grader, comparing new answers to earlier ones and judging which are more provable, efficient, or logical. As a result, GRPO is more scalable in situations where correctness is objective and high-quality human data is expensive or impractical to generate. 

A core idea behind GRPO is **relational feedback** [1]. Instead of labeling an answer as correct or incorrect, GRPO compares multiple outputs generated by the model and evaluates **relative quality**, rewarding improvements within groups of answers [1]. This makes it well suited for reasoning tasks where there are multiple valid outputs, but some are more efficient or intuitive than others, such as in **mathematical reasoning**. GRPO's ability to use self-correction that is consistent and automatic outperforms methods that rely on human feedback [1]. 

**Intuition:**  
Think of PPO as a teacher who tells you whether your answer is good or bad, and adjusts your approach accordingly. GRPO, by contrast, is like working with a group of your own drafts — you generate several answers, then learn by comparing them against each other, rewarding whichever version is better. This “compare within groups” setup avoids needing a teacher (human labels) and works especially well when correctness is clear-cut, like math proofs. 

## Mathematical explanation 

GRPO builds on the standard **policy gradient** approach from reinforcement learning, introduced by Schulman et al. [2]. This method updates parameters using gradient ascent to make better decisions more likely over time [2]: 

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s) \cdot A(s, a)]
$$

Where: 
- $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ is the probability of taking action **a** in state **s**, under the policy **π** parameterized by **θ**.
- $\log \pi_\theta(a|s)$ is the log probability, used to improve numerical stability in order to calculate gradients.
- $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)$ is the direction that would make the model more likely to choose action **a** in state **s**, as defined in PPO [2].
- $A(s, a)$ is the **advantage function**, measuring how much better this action is than what the model expected based on past experiences. 
- $\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$ means that we take the average over many samples.

**Intuition:**  
The advantage function is like giving credit only to the actions that worked better than expected, instead of rewarding everything equally. If an action was better than average, it gets amplified; if worse, it gets suppressed. This ensures learning focuses on what truly improved results. 

---

Similarly to PPO, GRPO uses a **clipping function** to control the degree of parameter updates and maintain stability. The clipping function, **first introduced in PPO by Schulman et al. [2]**, is applied to the probability ratio between new and old policies, restricting how much the new policy can deviate from the old one. As described by Schulman et al., this produces a "pessimistic bound" on policy updates: 

> “Finally, we take the minimum of the clipped and unclipped objective, so the final objective is a lower bound (i.e., a pessimistic bound) on the unclipped objective.”  
> — Schulman et al., *Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms*, arXiv:1707.06347, Section 3

The clipped objective function is: 

$$
L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \min \left( r_t A_t, \text{clip}(r_t, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) A_t \right) \right]
$$

Where:

$$
r_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)}
$$

In this:
- $r_t$ is the ratio between new and old policy probabilities. If $r_t$ is far from 1, it indicates that the new policy has large deviations from the original. 
- $\epsilon$ is a small number that defines how much deviation from the old policy is permitted per update.
- The $\text{clip}$ function ensures that **updates don't go too far** even when the advantage is high.

**Intuition:**  
Without clipping, the model might overreact to one lucky improvement and make drastic changes. Clipping keeps learning steady — like training with a safety harness so that every step forward is safe, even if the model finds a “too good to be true” action.  

---

GRPO **adapts this concept by applying clipping across groups of outputs**, not just individual ones. This enables optimization for comparative quality within sets of answers, rather than looking for absolute correctness alone [1].  

**Intuition:**  
You write five solutions to a math problem. Instead of asking “is each one right or wrong?”, GRPO asks “which ones are better than the others?” It then nudges the policy to produce more like the stronger ones. Clipping ensures these nudges don’t overcorrect.
